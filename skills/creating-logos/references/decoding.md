# Decoding a category, and finding what the mark must say

A contact sheet of competitor logos is not research. It tells you what the category
looks like and nothing about why — and a mark drawn from "what the category looks
like" is a shape with no argument behind it. This is the stage that turns a sheet
into a brief.

## Decode each competitor

For every direct competitor, answer these four in writing. Reconstructing the mark
yourself is the fastest way to see its skeleton; a rough redraw with the underlying
structure showing is worth more than any amount of staring.

| Question | What you are looking for |
| --- | --- |
| **What is it depicting?** | The literal object or action, before any interpretation |
| **How does that relate to the name?** | Which half of the name the mark spends itself on |
| **Is a letterform in there?** | Often yes — and usually as a *by-product* of drawing the idea, not as the idea |
| **How does the category's shared noun show up?** | The convention everyone repeats, which is therefore spent |

Then look for the pattern across all of them. There is almost always one, and it is
usually the brief.

**A worked example.** Five form and survey tools: form·sort draws unlike shapes put
in order; fill·out draws completed lines and a final dot; you·form draws a cursor,
because *you* are the half that is theirs; form·stack draws cards receding; jot·form
draws pen strokes at a handwriting slant. Every one of them spends the mark on **the
verb in its own name**, and lets the shared noun be plain horizontal bars, because
everyone already reads bars as fields.

That single sentence decided everything downstream: the shared-noun shorthand was
spent, and the brief was to draw the verb.

## Then find your own

**Start with the name.** Split it. Look up the root — if it has an etymology, the
brief is frequently sitting inside it, already agreed by whoever named the thing.
A name whose second half is a *verb* is a gift: draw the verb.

**Then the product's own loop**, from its own copy rather than your summary of it:

- What goes in, and what comes out
- The one claim it makes that nobody else in the category can
- What it is **anxious** about — the flank its marketing defends in two or three
  separate places. Whatever that is, the mark must not undermine it. A product
  fighting to be believed cannot afford a mark that reads as casual.
- How it talks: the register the identity has to match

## The output

One line saying what the mark depicts, then a short **must** and **must not** list
drawn from the above. Show it, get it agreed, and only then draw.

The must-nots are the valuable half, because they are specific rather than tasteful:
a shorthand four competitors already own, a lane a direct competitor sits in, a
finish that contradicts the product's own claim about itself.

## Two failure modes

**A letter puzzle.** Making the initials interlock is clever about *spelling* and
silent about *meaning*. Check the category first: if none of them does a letter
trick for its own sake, that is not an accident.

**Depicting the object instead of the operation.** The noun is what the category
already draws. The verb is what is left.
